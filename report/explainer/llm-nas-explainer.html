0Background

The paper and our two questions

What LLM-NAS does. It searches for small neural networks that are both accurate and fast on a given chip. The candidates are cells from NAS-Bench-201: each cell has six connections, and each connection is one of five operations, so there are 15,625 possible cells. Instead of changing cells at random, as an evolutionary algorithm would, the method asks GPT-4.1 to propose new cells from the best ones found so far. It also has the model keep a running list of design rules, the knowledge base.

Two other parts matter. The space is split into six niches by how many 3×3 convolutions a cell has, so the search covers simple and complex designs alike. And no network is trained: accuracy comes from a predictor built on cheap "zero-cost" scores, and latency from a table of measurements on six chips (HW-NAS-Bench).

What it claims. Fronts close to the best possible (hypervolume 0.997 on CIFAR-10, Edge GPU) from 120 API calls and about 3 minutes per search. The authors did not release code.

Our two questions

  1. With the same evaluator and the same budget, does the LLM find better designs than a plain, non-LLM search?
  2. Does the LLM bring design knowledge that the search would not have found by itself?
Why this framing

The paper compares itself with methods that train networks for days. Much of its saving comes from not training at all, which any search algorithm can do. To credit the LLM, it has to beat other searches that use the same shortcut.

1Done

Objective 1: a reproduction anyone can re-run

Can every number be regenerated later, without paying for API calls again?

  • Rebuilt the method from the paper's text, its Algorithm 1 and the prompts in its Appendix E, as a Python package.
  • Put every setting in one file, tagged [paper] if the paper states it or [assumed] if we had to choose (13 choices, such as 5 starting cells per niche and 4 children per call).
  • Gave each run a folder named after a fingerprint of its settings, so a finished run is never recomputed or overwritten.
  • Logged every prompt and reply. Any LLM search can be replayed exactly, offline and at no cost.
  • 61 automated tests run on synthetic data with a fake LLM.
  • One script, report/paper_numbers.py, computes every number in the paper's tables from the archived runs.
Now public

The code, settings, reports and every run's logs are at github.com/Zimozoxolo/llm-nas-repro under the MIT licence.

Objective 2: is our data the paper's data?

Before comparing results, do the benchmark tables we built match what the paper used?

We merged three public sources into one table per dataset (latency from HW-NAS-Bench, zero-cost scores from NAS-Bench-Suite-Zero, accuracy from NATS-Bench) and checked it against every number the paper prints.

  • 11 of the 12 architectures in the paper's Table 2 exist in the benchmark with matching accuracy and latency. The twelfth (94.37% at 4.35 ms on Edge GPU) does not exist; the same row is printed for another method, so it is probably a copying error.
  • A benchmark flaw. 4,699 cells (30%) have an Edge TPU latency of −1, meaning they failed to compile. Treated as real numbers they look like free, instant models and drag every Edge TPU score down to about 0.2. We treat them as impossible on that chip. The paper does not say what it did.
  • Our accuracy predictor ranks cells better than the paper's: a rank correlation of 0.96 against about 0.90. It helps every method equally.
What it means

The data matches, so differences from the paper come from the method or the scoring, not from the data.

Objective 5: is the scoring rule fair?

The paper does not say exactly how it computes hypervolume. Does the choice change who wins?

Hypervolume is the area between a set of designs and a reference point, reported as a share of the area the true best front covers, so 1.0 is perfect.

One useless cell distorts the scale

On Edge GPU the true best front ends in a "network" whose input never reaches its output. It answers at chance level (10% accuracy) but runs fastest (0.50 ms), so it sits on the front and squeezes all sensible designs into the top 9% of the accuracy scale.

Every one of the 50 control searches on Edge GPU ends with such a cell on its front. The LLMs almost never propose one, since they are asked for accurate models. Where an LLM search has one, it was usually already in the 30 random starting cells. That luck, not the model, is why Qwen's seeds score 0.888 without it and 0.985 and 0.975 with it.

What it means

Comparing HV across papers in this field is unreliable until authors state their scoring rule. Our main conclusion holds under all 16 rules. Claude Opus's lead depends on the rule and on one search.

Objective 6: is the LLM worth it?

With the same evaluator, does the LLM find better fronts, or find them with fewer evaluations, than a cheap search?

Equal evaluations

The LLM searches evaluate fewer cells because many proposals are rejected. To compare fairly we read each control at the point where it had evaluated as many cells as the LLM search, averaged over its ten seeds.

  • GPT-4.1 is level with random sampling and below the evolutionary searches.
  • Qwen and Haiku are below every control.
  • DeepSeek looks level with three controls, but only because it evaluates so few cells (63). At that point every method is barely past its random start, and two of DeepSeek's Edge GPU searches had the degenerate cell in that start.
  • Regularised evolution leads every LLM by 0.03–0.06.

Is the gap real or noise?

We tested whether the LLMs are worse than each control with a cautious test that treats the six devices as six data points; its smallest possible p-value is 0.016. Against NSGA-II, regularised evolution and PEA with refilling, the LLMs reach it under all 16 scoring rules.

What it costs

A GPT-4.1 search costs $0.80–0.88 and takes about 8 minutes; Haiku $1.44–1.62 and about 40 minutes; Opus $10.77 and 71 minutes; the local models nothing in fees but 1–3 hours each. The controls cost nothing and finish in seconds.

Where an LLM could still pay off (not tested)

Expensive evaluations

Here an evaluation takes a millisecond. If each one meant training a network, saving evaluations would matter.

Open-ended spaces

When designs are code, writing a random mutation is hard and a text interface helps.

Explanations

The rules and rationales are readable. Only Claude Opus backed its rules with its own measurements.

Larger spaces

15,625 cells is small enough for random search to do well. A good prior might matter more in a far larger space.

What it means

On this benchmark the paper's cost saving comes from skipping training, which the free controls also do. The LLM adds readable rules and a text interface, not better fronts.

Objective 7: where the budget goes

Why do the LLM searches evaluate so few cells?

The paper's budget is 120 calls: 60 update the knowledge base and 60 ask for 4 new cells each, so 240 cells are requested. A rejected proposal is not replaced, so GPT-4.1 evaluates about 145 cells (30 starting cells plus the accepted ones), against 270 for the searches that keep generating until the budget is full.

Two different failures

  • Copying what they are shown. 58% of DeepSeek's proposals and 33% of Haiku's are a parent from the same prompt, returned unchanged.
  • Forgetting what they tried. GPT-4.1 rarely copies a parent (6%). Most of its duplicates (32% of all its proposals) are cells it had seen several generations earlier, which have since dropped out of the prompt.
  • For GPT-4.1 the duplicate rate climbs from 3% in generation 1 to 65% by generations 8–10. Running out of space is not the cause: at most 4.2% of a niche had been evaluated, on average, when a proposal was made.
What it means

Part of the LLM's lower score could come from evaluating fewer cells, not only from worse proposals. The next test separates the two.

Objective 8: the pre-registered duplicate-fix test

If GPT-4.1 is shown what it already tried, and asked again when it repeats itself, does it stop wasting proposals and catch up?

The questions, rules, pass marks and expected result were written and committed before any paid call. Two amendments were made, dated, before any arm result existed.

Arm A: history + ask again

The prompt lists up to 45 cells already evaluated in the niche. Rejected children are asked for again, with the reason, up to 5 times. Never padded with random cells. Budget: 270 evaluations. Cost $14.07.

Arm B: history only

The same list, nothing else, and the paper's 120 calls. Cost $5.53.

What it means

The repeats come from the model's proposals, not from missing information. The objection that the LLM loses only because it evaluates fewer cells is not supported.

9Paper

What the updated paper concludes

The data is recoverable, with two caveats: one reported point does not exist, and the Edge TPU column hides 4,699 failed compilations.

The scoring rule is the biggest unknown. One degenerate cell decides how compressed the scores are; until authors state their rule, HV values from different papers can't be compared.

No LLM search beats the strong non-LLM searches on any of the six devices (0 of 48), under any of the 16 scoring rules. Only Claude Opus, under the 80% floor and from one $11 search, edges ahead on Edge GPU.

The cost saving comes from the training-free evaluator, which every search can use, not from the language model.

Wasted proposals are not the reason the LLM loses. The pre-registered fixes let GPT-4.1 evaluate more cells, but not catch up.

Limits: one small, tabulated search space with a cheap evaluator; one seed for the API models; CIFAR-10 only for the LLMs.

GReference

Glossary

Cell
One candidate network design in NAS-Bench-201: six connections, each with one of five operations. 15,625 in total.
Pareto front
The designs for which nothing else is both faster and more accurate. The "true front" is the best possible one, computed from the full benchmark.
Hypervolume (HV)
The area covered by a set of designs up to a reference point, as a share of the true front's area. 1.0 is perfect.
IGD
Average distance from the true front to the nearest found design. Lower is better.
Niche
One of six slices of the space, by number of 3×3 convolutions. Each niche is searched separately.
Operator
The step that makes new designs from old ones. LLM-NAS uses an LLM; evolutionary search uses random mutation and crossover.
PEA
The paper's own ablation: the same framework with random edits instead of the LLM. Rejected children are lost.
PEA with refilling
PEA that redraws a rejected child until the budget of 270 evaluations is full.
NSGA-II, regularised evolution
Two standard evolutionary algorithms, run with the same predictor and 270 evaluations.
Zero-cost predictor
A model that estimates accuracy from cheap scores computed without training, so a design is scored in about a millisecond.
Seed
One independent repeat of a search with a different random start.
Degenerate cell
A cell whose input never reaches its output. It guesses at chance level but is very fast, so it can sit on the true front and distort the scale.
80% floor
Scoring that ignores every design below 80% accuracy, which removes the degenerate cell.
Sign-flip test
A significance test on the six per-device differences; with six devices the smallest possible p-value is 1/64 = 0.016.
Pre-registration
Writing the test and its pass/fail rules down, and committing them, before seeing results.
